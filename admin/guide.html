01

The dashboard

Signing in lands you on Home. It exists to answer one question before anything else: what needs me right now? If you only ever look at one screen, look at this one.

The row of numbers

Five counts of work that is actually waiting — follow-ups overdue, follow-ups due today, tasks overdue, tasks due today, and leads nobody has spoken to yet. A count of zero goes grey and does nothing. Any other number is a button, and clicking it takes you to exactly those records with the filter already applied.

Today

Your follow-ups and your tasks in one list, worst first — whatever is most overdue at the top, then everything due today. Leads and tasks are mixed together deliberately, because your morning does not separate them either. Click any row to open it.

Everything else

Buttons through to the other five screens, each with a live line underneath: how many leads are live, how many tasks are overdue, what you have spent on advertising. It is a menu that also tells you whether you need to go there.

Last 30 days, and Lately

A short version of the Analytics screen — new leads, closings, first-contact time, pipeline and spend — with a button through to the full thing. Underneath, the last few entries logged against any lead, so you can see what has been happening without going looking. These are computed by the same code the Analytics screen uses, so the two can never quietly disagree.

Getting around

The six screens live in the sidebar down the left. A number beside one is work that is already overdue, so the nav tells you where to look before you click anything.

Collapse at the bottom of the sidebar narrows it to a strip of icons and hands the width back to whatever you are looking at — worth doing on the lead board and the table. The overdue counts become dots, hovering an icon names it, and it stays how you leave it. On a phone the sidebar folds away entirely behind the ☰ button.

The five-minute morning

Open Home. If the top row is all zeros, you are clear. If it is not, work the Today list from the top until it is. Everything else in this guide is detail underneath that.

02

Finding anything

The box along the top searches everything at once — every lead, every task, every campaign — not just whatever screen you happen to be on. Results are grouped by what they are, and clicking one opens that record wherever it lives.

Leads
Name, email, phone, what they're looking to do, tags, your private notes, and the message they sent.
Tasks
Title, notes, who it's for, and the lead or campaign it's attached to.
Campaigns
Name, channel, area, ad copy and notes.
Go to
The screens themselves — type "analytics" to jump there.

Every word has to match somewhere in the record, so dana bayshore finds the Dana whose notes mention Bayshore and nothing else. Matching text is highlighted so you can see why something came back.

  • Ctrl + K (or ⌘ K) jumps to the search box from anywhere.
  • / does the same, as long as you are not already typing in a field.
  • ↑ ↓ move through results, Enter opens one, Esc clears.

This is not the same as the filters

The search box on the Leads screen filters the board you are looking at — it narrows a view. The box at the top finds one specific thing across the whole system and takes you to it. Use the filters to work a list; use the top box when you know who you are looking for.

03

When a lead arrives

Someone fills in the form on the website. Three things happen straight away:

  • The lead is saved to the board, in the New column.
  • You get an email with their details.
  • Their answers and where they came from are captured — nothing to retype.

Nothing is lost if something breaks

Saving the lead and sending the email happen independently. If one fails the other still goes through, so a lead is never dropped because a mail server was having a bad morning.

04

Working the board

Six columns, left to right, following a deal as it progresses:

StageMeans
NewArrived, not yet spoken to
ContactedYou've reached out
TouringActively looking at homes with you
Under ContractOffer accepted, working to close
ClosedDone. Keep them — past clients refer.
Lost / ColdNot moving forward, or gone quiet

To move someone: drag the card on a computer, or use the dropdown at the bottom of the card on a phone. Both do the same thing — dragging is fiddly on a touchscreen, so the dropdown is there for that.

The counters along the top always reflect what you're currently filtered to, not the whole database. Filter to Facebook leads and the pipeline figure is the Facebook pipeline.

Pipeline excludes Closed and Lost

Money already banked isn't pipeline, and neither is money that walked away. Counting either would flatter the number into uselessness.

06

Logging calls and notes

Two different things, for two different jobs.

The activity log

Pick what happened — Call, Text, Email, Showing, Offer or Note — write a line, and it's stamped with the date and kept newest first. This is the history of the relationship.

Private notes

A free-form box for things that aren't events — budget context, what they're really after, the fact that they hate cul-de-sacs. + Add dated entry drops today's date at the top if you prefer to keep it as a journal.

Why bother

Six months from now, "spoke once, seemed keen" is worth nothing. "Called 14 Mar — wants to be in before the school year, pre-approved to 480" is a deal you can pick straight back up.

07

Follow-ups

Leads rarely die because they weren't interested. They die because nobody called back. Set Next follow-up on every lead you intend to keep.

  • The card shows a badge as the date approaches, and turns orange when overdue.
  • The Overdue counter is your daily to-do count.
  • The follow-up filter narrows to Overdue, Due today, Due this week or No date set — check that last one weekly, since a lead with no date is a lead nobody has promised to chase.
08

Table view

The Board / Table switch shows the same leads differently. The board is for moving one person along; the table is for scanning everyone at once.

  • Click a column heading to sort, click again to reverse. Budget sorts by value, not alphabetically.
  • The boxes under the headings filter that column, and they stack — "hot sellers from referrals" is three clicks.
  • Click a row to open the same panel as a card.
  • The line at the bottom tells you what you're looking at, so a filtered view is never mistaken for the whole pipeline.

Your view, sort and filters are remembered, so it opens the way you left it.

Tasks

Pick Tasks in the sidebar. The lead board tracks people; this tracks work — the survey that has to be ordered, the appraisal nobody has chased, the photographer who needs booking on Tuesday.

It is the same kind of board, with four columns:

ColumnWhat belongs there
To doNot started
In progressYou're on it today
Waiting onOut of your hands — the lender, the inspector, the client. This column is the one that earns its keep: it stops "waiting" quietly becoming "forgotten"
DoneFinished. Only the twenty most recent stay on screen

Drag a card between columns, or use the dropdown at the bottom of it. Click a card to open it.

One task

  1. + New taskWrite it as an action you could start: Call the Alvarez family about the inspection, not Alvarez.
  2. Set a due datePrefilled to today. Anything past due turns orange on the card and is counted in Overdue at the top.
  3. Who it's forLeave it as your name, or type someone else's. Names you've used before come up as suggestions.
  4. Attach it to a lead or a campaignOptional, and worth doing — the card then shows whose deal it belongs to, and you can filter down to just that.

A whole job at once

Start a workflow lays out every step of a job you do the same way every time, already dated and in order:

New lead follow-up
Seven steps across the first fortnight, from the five-minute call to the decision to nurture or let go.
New listing launch
Eight steps from signed agreement to the first week's feedback.
Under contract → closing
Ten steps over thirty days. Every date here is one that costs money or the deal if it slips.
Open house
Nine days built around one Sunday — including the follow-up afterwards, which is where the leads actually come from.
Campaign launch
Getting an ad live, then actually judging it at day three, seven and fourteen.

Pick one, set the date it starts from, attach it to a lead or campaign, and you'll see exactly what you're about to get before you create it. Afterwards they are ordinary tasks — edit, re-date or delete any of them.

Why a closing checklist is worth the two minutes

A thirty-day contract has about ten hard deadlines in it, and missing one is not a tidiness problem — it is an escrow deposit, a rate lock, or a client's move-out date. Laying them all out on day one means the only thing you have to remember afterwards is to look at the board.

Finding things

  • Search covers titles, notes, who it's for, and the lead or campaign it's attached to.
  • Anyone narrows to one person — useful the moment more than one of you is working the board.
  • Any due date gives you overdue, today, this week, or the ones nobody has dated.
  • Anything attached separates deal work from campaign work from everything else.
11

Generate

Three jobs behind one tab strip: pages that capture leads, a ranking of who to call next, and the places leads arrive from.

Landing pages

A one-page site built around a single ask — a home valuation, a buyer guide, a neighbourhood report. Point an ad, a postcard QR code or a social post at it and every enquiry lands on your board already tagged with the campaign.

  1. + New pageName it, and the web address fills itself in. You can edit the address until you are happy — after that, leave it alone, because it is printed on things.
  2. Write the headline and bulletsOne bullet per line. Say what they get and what it costs them, which is nothing.
  3. Choose what to ask forEvery extra field costs you some submissions. Phone and timeframe usually pay for themselves; property address only on a valuation page.
  4. Attach a campaign, then set it LiveA draft returns a "not found" to the public, so you can build it in the open.

Views and conversion are on the card

Each page shows how many people saw it and how many filled it in. Under about 10% the page is not the problem so much as the traffic — check who you are sending.

Who to call next

Two ranked lists over the people already in your database. Most likely to move leans mostly on tenure: the window opens around year five after you closed someone and is widest either side of year ten. Buyers worth a call today ranks live leads by how far along and how engaged they are — showings logged, budget known, follow-ups that have slipped.

Every row shows why it scored what it did. If a reason looks wrong, the fix is in the lead record, not the score.

This reads your database, not the market

It ranks people you have actually dealt with, using things this system recorded. It does not look at strangers, property records, or anything describing who someone is. Scoring a whole neighbourhood needs an MLS or property-data feed this site does not have — and predicting moves from life events or anything standing in for a protected class is a fair-housing problem, not just a data one. The last card on that tab spells out both.

Lead sources

Each source gets its own private web address. Anything that can send a web request can post a lead to it, and it arrives on the board tagged with the right source and campaign. Field names are matched loosely, so name, full_name and firstName all work without mapping.

Facebook / Instagram
Zapier or Make: trigger on a new lead from your Page, action "Webhooks → POST" to the address. The cleanest of the three, because Meta genuinely pushes the lead.
Zillow
Zillow emails or texts each lead and only offers a direct feed to CRMs it has partnered with. Point a Zapier email-parser mailbox at the notification and have it POST the parsed fields here.
Realtor.com and others
Same email-parser route.
Anything else
An open-house iPad, a Google Form, another website's form — if it can POST, it works.

Treat the address like a password

Anyone who has it can add leads to your board. Do not paste it anywhere public. If it does get out, New address invalidates the old one immediately — remember to update whatever was posting to it.

CMA

Price a property from comparable sales, then hand the seller a report that shows your working. This is the listing-appointment tool.

  1. Type the address and hit Look upIt confirms the property against county records — folio, ZIP, last recorded sale — and lists recent sales within half a mile, nearest first, for you to add as comps in one click.
  2. Fill in what county records do not holdBeds, baths, living area, lot, year and condition are not published by the property appraiser. They come from your MLS, and they are what the adjustments run on. Nothing will guess them for you.
  3. Check what each difference is worthThe defaults are ordinary Florida figures, not quotes. Change them to what your market actually pays.
  4. Read the grid, then the indicated valueIt updates as you type. Add a note about anything the numbers do not say.
  5. Download report or Print / PDFDownload gives you a single file you can email. Print opens it ready for Save as PDF.

What the lookup can and cannot reach

It uses the US Census geocoder and the county property appraiser's own public data. Those give the verified address, the folio, the ZIP and every recent sale nearby with its price and date — the tedious half. They do not publish bedroom counts, square footage or year built, so those stay yours to enter. Hillsborough County is wired up; other counties confirm the address but return no parcel or sales.

Filling that last gap automatically needs an MLS data feed. Stellar offers one to members who apply for a licence — that is a form you submit, not something this site can arrange, and once you have the credentials the comp rows can fill themselves.

How the adjustments work

Every comp is adjusted to the subject. Where a comp is worse, its price is adjusted up — it sold for less than the subject should. Where it is better, down. The adjusted figures are what the comps say the subject is worth.

Living area is adjusted at about half the market rate per square foot, which is deliberate: a bigger house costs more, but not proportionally. Leave that field blank and it works itself out from your comps.

Least-adjusted wins

The reconciled value is weighted towards whichever comps needed the least adjusting, because those are the strongest evidence. A comp needing 25% gross adjustment barely moves the number, and it should not — if you have to change that much about a property, it was not really comparable.

A CMA is not an appraisal

The report says so in writing, on the page, every time. It is an opinion of value from a licensed agent, not a state-certified appraiser, it does not comply with USPAP, and it cannot be the basis for a mortgage. Do not remove that paragraph and do not let anyone describe the document as an appraisal — that is a licensing problem, not a wording preference.

Campaigns

Pick Campaigns in the sidebar. This plans advertising and — the useful part — shows which of it actually produces business.

  1. + New campaignName it something you'll recognise in six months: Spring sellers — South Tampa, not Campaign 3.
  2. Set channel, goal and areaFacebook, direct mail, open house, print. Buyers or sellers. Then where.
  3. Enter your budgetWhat you're prepared to spend.
  4. Save and copy the tracking linkThe next section explains why this matters.
  5. Update "Spent so far" as you goNo connection to your ad account, so this is manual — a minute a week.
FigureWhat it tells you
SpendWhat you've put in so far
LeadsPeople who enquired via that campaign's link
ClosedHow many of those actually transacted
Per leadSpend ÷ leads — what each enquiry cost

Closed is the column that matters

Facebook can tell you it delivered cheap clicks. It has no idea which ones bought a house. Because your leads and your ads live in the same place here, you can see that the $8-a-lead channel produced nothing and the $40-a-lead channel paid for the year.

14

The tracking link

Every campaign gets its own web address when you save it:

https://jessicakortum.com/?utm_campaign=spring-sellers-a1b2

Use that instead of the plain website address anywhere the campaign appears — the ad's destination, a QR code on a postcard, a link in an email.

It looks like the normal site to whoever clicks. But when they enquire, the lead is quietly tagged with that campaign, which is how the Leads and Closed columns fill in.

What it won't catch

Someone who sees the ad and rings you instead of using the form won't be counted automatically — ask how they heard of you and set the Source by hand.

The tag also lasts for that visit only. Click the ad today, come back next week and enquire then, and it won't be attributed. That's deliberate — the alternative credits old ads for business they didn't bring in — but treat the numbers as a floor rather than an exact count.

15

Fair housing — read this once

Housing ads are legally restricted

Since the 2019 HUD settlement, Facebook, Instagram and Google prohibit housing advertisers from targeting by age, gender, ZIP code or detailed demographics, and require a minimum 15-mile radius. Those details stand in for protected characteristics — and having children is one of them. This applies to you as a licensed agent, not just to the platform.

In practice: describe places and intentions, never people.

Instead ofWrite
Young families with kids in WestchaseWestchase and the surrounding area
Retirees looking to downsizeHomeowners considering a smaller property
Newlyweds buying their first homeFirst-time buyers

As you type in the audience or ad copy boxes, anything describing people rather than places is flagged in orange. It's a prompt, not a block — "family room" is a feature, not an audience, so it lets you save and leaves the judgement to you.

Set the actual audience inside Facebook or Google, where the platform enforces the rules for you. This tool plans and measures; it doesn't buy.

17

Analytics

Pick Analytics in the sidebar. It reads your leads, tasks and campaigns together and tells you what they add up to. Pick a period in the top left — 30 days, 90 days, 12 months or everything.

The six numbers at the top

New leads
How many arrived, and whether that is up or down on the period before. Everything else is downstream of this one.
Closed
Deals that reached Closed during the period.
Win rate
Closed against closed-plus-lost. Only counts leads that actually resolved, so leads still in play do not drag it down.
Median first contact
How long a lead waits for your first call, text or email. The most actionable number on the page.
Open pipeline
The budgets on every live lead added up. Leads with no budget set are not counted, so it is a floor rather than a forecast.
Enquiry to closing
How long a deal takes end to end, from the enquiry landing to the keys.

Where leads stop

A funnel of the leads that arrived in the period, showing how far each has got. The line under each row says how many were lost at that step. One big drop-off tells you more about what to fix than any other chart here — a wall between Contacted and Touring is a different problem from one between Under Contract and Closed.

How fast you answer

Your leads sorted into within an hour, same day, 1–3 days and longer. There is a fifth bar, Nothing logged, and it is the honest one: this whole card is built from your contact log, so a call you made but never logged shows up here as a lead you ignored.

The page tells you when it is guessing

If most leads have nothing logged, the card says so rather than quietly reporting a median from a third of your data. Same with older closings — the board only started recording stage moves partway through its life, so deals that closed before that are dated by when the record was last touched, and the page flags it.

Where business comes from, and what advertising produced

Sources are ranked by closings, not by volume — the point is to show where your time actually pays, which is rarely where the most leads are.

The campaign table is deliberately all time, even when the period selector says 30 days. Spend is a running total with no date attached to it, so dividing it by a month of leads would produce a cost-per-lead that looks precise and is wrong.

What to do about it

The last card turns the numbers into a short list of things to actually do, and each one only appears when your data shows it — overdue tasks, leads with no follow-up date, a campaign spending money with nothing attributed, a source producing volume but no closings. Several have a button that takes you straight to the leads or tasks in question with the right filter already applied.

Copy summary puts the whole thing on the clipboard as plain text, which is handy for a monthly note to yourself or your broker.

Nothing here segments people

This page measures where leads came from and what happened next. It does not, and must not, break your leads down by anything describing the people themselves. Fair housing explains why that line matters.

18

Calculators

Pick Calculators in the sidebar. Five of them, all running in the browser — nothing is sent anywhere, so you can open one on a client's kitchen table without thinking about it.

Monthly payment
Principal, interest, taxes, insurance and HOA. The Florida insurance figure is the one clients most often underestimate, so it is editable rather than assumed.
Affordability
Works backwards from income and debts to a price range. Useful for the "what should we even be looking at?" conversation before a lender is involved.
Seller net
Sale price minus commission, title, doc stamps and payoff. What actually lands in their account.
Cash to close
Down payment plus closing costs plus prepaids, less the deposit already down.
Investment
Rent against costs — cash flow, cap rate and cash-on-cash for a rental.

Every rate and percentage is editable. The defaults are ordinary Florida figures, not quotes.

Estimates, not documents

None of these is a Loan Estimate, a title commitment or a closing statement. Tax, insurance and title numbers vary by county, lender and insurer, and Florida homeowners insurance moves fast. Use them to shape a conversation; confirm with the lender and the title company before anyone relies on a figure.

19

A working routine

Every morning — five minutes

  • Open Home and work the Today list from the top until the counts across the top are zero.
  • Anything in New gets a call the same day. Speed is most of the battle.
  • In Tasks, move today's work into In progress so you know what you actually committed to.
  • Log what happened as you go, not on Friday when you've forgotten.

Every week — fifteen minutes

  • Filter follow-up to No date set and give every one a date.
  • Read the Waiting on column and chase anything that has sat there more than a few days. Nobody else is going to.
  • Update Spent so far on any running campaign.
  • Compare Per lead and Closed across campaigns. Put more into what works, stop what doesn't.
  • Move anything genuinely dead to Lost / Cold.

Every month

  • Open Analytics, set the period to 90 days, and read the What to do about it card top to bottom. That is the whole review.
  • Export CSV and keep the file somewhere safe.
  • Look through Closed — those are your referral sources, and the cheapest business you'll ever get.
